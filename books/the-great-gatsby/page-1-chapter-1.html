Chapter 1: The Green Light

In my younger and more vulnerable years, my father gave me some advice that I've been turning over in my mind ever since. "Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone," he told me, "just remember that all the people in this world haven't had the advantages that you've had."

I, Nick Carraway, am inclined to reserve all judgments, a habit that has opened up many curious natures to me but also made me the victim of not a few veteran bores. After returning from the Great War, I decided to move East and learn the bond business. I settled in West Egg, a less fashionable commuter town on Long Island, in a small weather-beaten cardboard bungalow.

My house was squeezed between two huge places that rented for twelve or fifteen thousand a season. The one on my right was a colossal affair by any standard—it was a factual imitation of some Hotel de Ville in Normandy, with a tower on one side, spanking new under a thin beard of raw ivy, and a marble swimming pool, and more than forty acres of lawn and garden. It was Gatsby's mansion.

Across the courtesy bay the white palaces of fashionable East Egg glittered along the water. I drove over there to have dinner with the Tom Buchanans. Daisy was my second cousin once removed, and I’d known Tom in college. Tom Buchanan was a sturdy straw-haired man of thirty with a rather hard mouth and a supercilious manner. They were enormously wealthy—even in college his freedom with money was a matter for reproach.

The Buchanans lived in a cheerful red-and-white Georgian Colonial mansion, overlooking the bay. Inside, the windows were ajar and gleaming white against the fresh grass outside. Daisy Buchanan and her friend Jordan Baker were buoyed up as though upon an anchored balloon. Daisy murmured, "I'm p-paralyzed with happiness."

Dinner was a rich, cynical affair. Tom expounded on "The Rise of the Colored Empires," revealing his deep-seated racism. The phone rang—it was Tom's mistress in New York, a fact Jordan casually revealed to me. "She might have the decency not to telephone him at dinner time. Don't you think?" Jordan asked.

After dinner, Daisy spoke to me on the porch. Her voice glowed and sang. She told me about her daughter's birth. "I woke up out of the ether with an utterly abandoned feeling, and asked the nurse right away if it was a boy or a girl. She told me it was a girl, and so I turned my head away and wept. 'All right,' I said, 'I'm glad it's a girl. And I hope she'll be a fool—that's the best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful little fool.'"

I returned home confused and a little disgusted. As I sat out on my porch to look at the sea, I saw a figure emerge from the shadow of my neighbor's mansion. It was Gatsby. He stretched out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way, and, far as I was from him, I could have sworn he was trembling. Involuntarily I glanced seaward—and distinguished nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock.
